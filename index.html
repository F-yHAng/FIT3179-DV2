More recorded home users. A smaller population share. Many different paths forward.

Australia's First Nations include Aboriginal peoples and Torres Strait Islander peoples—diverse communities with many languages.

For readers in multilingual Malaysia, the distinction matters: knowing a language, using it at home and learning it are different things.

This story follows recorded home use, looks across generations, and turns to communities working to sustain their languages.

01

More users, a smaller share

Between 1991 and 2021, the Census recorded more Indigenous-language home users. The Indigenous population grew faster, so the share using these languages at home declined.

+77%

Recorded home users increased by about 77%, while their population share fell by 6.9 percentage points.

These figures describe Census records. Population growth, identification, classification and question changes can influence the trend. In 2021, “speak” became “use”, including sign-language users.
02

Geography changes the picture

A high local share and a large number of home users answer different questions. Compare the same eight states and territories through both lenses.

Reading Australia: NSW = New South Wales; VIC = Victoria; QLD = Queensland; SA = South Australia; WA = Western Australia; TAS = Tasmania; NT = Northern Territory; ACT = Australian Capital Territory. These are administrative boundaries, not traditional language territories. Other Territories are omitted from the maps but retained in the published national total.

The national pattern does not describe every state.

NT's share rose from 54.0% to 58.5%. NSW's share rose from 0.6% to 1.9%, while WA's share edged down.

Both endpoints include all Indigenous residents in their denominators. The 2021 question used “use” in place of “speak”.
03

Many languages, different paths

The national count combines many named categories. Some have thousands of recorded home users; many have only a few. Creoles, including Yumplatok and Kriol, are part of this statistical landscape.

“Named categories” are ABS statistical labels: they may represent languages, dialects or creoles. Not further defined and not elsewhere classified records are excluded here because they do not identify a single named category. The 159 figure is therefore not a count of all Australian languages.

05

Communities are keeping languages in use

The fourth National Indigenous Languages Survey (NILS4), conducted in 2025–26, offers a new view of current language strength and revival. Its definitions and coverage differ from earlier surveys.

A new baseline: NILS4 changes in definitions, response coverage and strength assessment prevent a simple growth-rate comparison with NILS3. The 45 strong languages and 113 revival languages are not mutually exclusive categories.

Keeping a language alive involves its use in everyday life, its place across generations, and the communities sustaining and rebuilding it.

No single count captures all of these. The Census and language surveys offer different views—useful together, provided their differences remain visible.

How to read these measures

Home use: Indigenous people reporting an Indigenous language at home, based on usual residence and excluding overseas visitors. The population-share denominator includes people whose language response was not stated. This differs from knowing a language, using it outside home or fluency.

Statistical categories: named labels can include languages, dialects and creoles. Unspecified/residual categories are excluded from the named-category views but remain in national totals. Census confidentiality adjustments can cause small discrepancies between cell sums and totals. Ranking comparisons retain the same 196 labels in 2011, 2016 and 2021.

Survey status: NILS4 records survey-covered language varieties, not an exhaustive count of all languages. State associations can overlap. Language strength is not derived from Census home-user thresholds.
