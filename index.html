More recorded home users. A smaller population share. Many different paths forward.

Australia’s First Nations include Aboriginal peoples and Torres Strait Islander peoples, with many different languages.

Think of a multilingual home in Malaysia: someone may know several languages but use one most often at home. They may also be learning another language. Knowing, using at home and learning describe different things.

The National Indigenous Languages Survey (NILS4) asks communities about language use, strength and revival.

More users, a smaller share

03

Many languages, different paths

Places tell only part of the story. Some languages have thousands of home users; many have only a few. The Census includes creoles such as Yumplatok and Kriol.

Counts tell us how many; age patterns tell us more about who.

04

Who is using these languages?

The next question is who uses these languages. Start with age patterns within languages, then look at the changing national age mix.

The Census shows home use; community surveys add another view of language life.

05

Communities are keeping languages in use

Home use is one view of language life. The 2025–26 survey adds community reports of language strength and revival, alongside recorded service locations.

What to remember

  • More people can use a language at home even while their share of the population falls.
  • Home use varies widely across Australia, and a high local share differs from a large number of users.
  • The Census, community survey and service map offer different, complementary views of language life.

What the measures cover

Home use: The Census counts Indigenous usual residents who report using an Indigenous language at home, excluding overseas visitors. This differs from fluency, knowing a language or using it elsewhere. National and state percentages compare home users with Indigenous residents, including people who gave no language answer.

Language names: Census entries may describe languages, dialects or creoles. Views of individual languages leave out answers that do not identify a particular language; national totals retain them. Privacy adjustments can make added counts differ slightly from published totals.

Community survey: NILS4 covers the language varieties reported to the survey, not every Australian language. Its questions, coverage and strength assessment differ from earlier surveys, so differences in totals do not directly show improvement. We use reported results, not future policy targets.

Maps: We simplified boundary shapes to reduce downloads. State borders are administrative areas, not traditional language areas. Circles summarise states; service dots show recorded locations. Neither identifies individual people.
